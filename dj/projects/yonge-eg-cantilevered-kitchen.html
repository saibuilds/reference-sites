Yonge & Eg · Cantilevered Kitchen

Walnut slab cabinetry, paneled Sub-Zero, and a Calacatta quartz waterfall island floated on a six-foot steel cantilever.

— The Story of the Build —

The brief was four words long: make it float, and make it last.

The clients bought a 1962 modernist semi off Roehampton in late 2022 and lived with the original galley kitchen for sixteen months before they called us. The husband is a structural engineer who designs long-span timber arenas. The wife is a documentary filmmaker who edits at the kitchen counter because, in her words, the rest of the house is too quiet. They knew what they wanted before we met. They wanted a walnut island that read as a single solid object, with no visible support on the eating side, so that two stools could sit there and so that a child could one day swing their legs without hitting a leg.

The constraint was the slab. The original kitchen sat directly above the basement furnace room with no headroom to lower a beam. The engineer-husband sketched the solution himself: a six-foot cantilever in a welded HSS frame, hidden inside the cabinetry stack on the wall side, transferring the moment back to the existing party wall through a steel knife plate. We had the steel fabricated in Vaughan, delivered in three pieces, and stitch-welded on site over two days. The cantilever is rated for twelve hundred pounds at the free end. The Calacatta quartz waterfall, fabricated as a single sixteen-foot piece by Ciot, weighs eight hundred and forty.

The casework is walnut slab on a poplar substrate, finished with a hand-rubbed conversion varnish in three coats. We chose to expose the grain rather than book-match it because the wife wanted the cabinetry to read as planks of a single tree, not as wallpaper. The hardware is unlacquered brass from a small shop in Detroit; it will tarnish unevenly over the next decade, which is the point. The backsplash is a single slab of the same Calacatta, returned up the wall and around the window jamb in a continuous run that took the stone setter eleven hours and three apprentices.

The Sub-Zero is paneled and integrated; the Wolf range is the only piece of metal in the room that announces itself. The filmmaker now edits at the cantilevered end of the island most mornings. The engineer, when asked at the final walk what he thought of the load test, said only: nothing moved, which is what we wanted.
