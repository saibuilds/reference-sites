Don Mills · Speakeasy Basement

A moody lower-level rebuild with rift oak panelling, a leather banquette, a hidden bar door, and a single-glass garage door to the garden.
DON MILLS, TORONTOBASEMENT · 980 SFWHISKEY LOUNGE · 14 WK

A lower level that holds a crowd.

A wine importer and his partner, owners of a 1958 Don Mills bungalow that wanted a second room of its own
Location
Don Mills, north Toronto

— The Story of the Build —

A speakeasy is a room that pretends it isn’t there. The trick is to make the pretending feel like generosity, not deception.

The client imports natural wine for restaurants from the Niagara escarpment and the Loire. He used to host monthly tastings in the dining room of his 1958 Don Mills bungalow, and when the guest list grew past sixteen he and his partner decided the dining room was no longer the right room. They wanted the lower level. They wanted it to read as if it had always been there, with no daylight asked for and no apology made.

The starting point was bad. The original basement was a 1980s rec room with a Berber carpet, fluorescent troffers, six feet of clear height, and a cinder-block laundry corner that smelled, faintly, of cat. We underpinned the perimeter by sixteen inches to bring the finished ceiling to seven feet two, replaced the slab with a vapour-broken pour, and ran new sanitary through a sealed pit and pump. The structural drawings were stamped by Vrang in week three. The underpin took twenty-one working days.

The lounge wall is rift white oak in two-and-a-half-inch vertical slats over a one-quarter rabbet shadow line, finished in a custom stain we matched to a single bottle of Hibiki the client brought to the design meeting. The banquette is full-grain bridle leather from a saddlery in Aylmer, hand-tufted with brass nail-heads, sized for nine adults comfortably or eleven if they have been drinking. The bar is a four-foot run of book-matched soapstone with a hidden pivot door at the back that opens onto the powder room. The door is invisible until pushed; the test, the client says, is whether a first-time guest finds it before their second drink.

The single concession to daylight is a glass garage-door panel on the south wall, eight feet wide, lifted by a counter-weighted bottom-up mechanism. In summer it folds up under the joists and the lounge spills onto a flagstone pad in the back garden. In winter it stays closed and you watch the snow fall on the cedar fence while the wine warms in your hand. We finished on a Friday in late October. The first tasting was the following Saturday. Eleven adults, six bottles, no one found the bar door until after the second pour.
