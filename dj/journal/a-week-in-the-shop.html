A week in the shop.

Five days, four jobs, one broken planer blade. What an actual week at our Etobicoke bench looks like, hour by honest hour.
BY SAI NATHAN

People who do not work in a custom-cabinet shop think the work is mostly the building. People who do work in one know the work is mostly the sequencing — which job is on the bench, which is in the spray booth, which is on the truck, and which one is sitting on a pallet waiting on a backordered hinge. The bench-time itself is maybe forty per cent of the week. The rest is sequencing, deliveries, three phone calls per job, and the small daily emergency that you did not see coming on Monday morning. This is one real week from our Etobicoke shop in early April. Names of the clients have been left out. Everything else is as it happened.

Monday.

Seven-fifteen. Shop unlocked. Coffee on. Compressor on. The two carpenters who run our day-shift are in by seven-thirty. The first hour is what we call "yesterday's list" — the dozen small things from Friday afternoon that nobody had the energy to finish. A drawer front that needs the second coat of finish, a hinge plate that needs to be re-drilled, two filler strips that need to be ripped to a fussy width for an install on Wednesday.

Nine. The walnut for the Yonge-and-Eg kitchen comes off the truck. Eighty-two board feet, kiln-dried to nine per cent, milled to eight-quarter. We rack it in the back of the shop on stickered sleepers and we do not touch it for forty-eight hours. The wood needs to acclimate to the shop's humidity before any saw goes near it. This is not optional. Skipping this step is how you build a cabinet that opens fine in April and binds in August.

Eleven to four-thirty. Bench-time on the Davisville island. Three drawer boxes, the under-counter pull-out, the panel that wraps the dishwasher. Standard work, four hours of focus, no surprises.

Tuesday.

Eight. The planer blade goes. Not catastrophically — it just nicks badly enough on a hidden mineral inclusion in a hard-maple board that the next twenty boards come off the planer with a ridge that has to be sanded out by hand. We change the blade at nine-fifteen. We lose maybe ninety minutes. This is normal. This happens roughly twice a month. If the planer never nicks a blade, the planer is not earning its keep.

The shop runs on the assumption that something will break before noon on Tuesday. The week that nothing breaks is the week we forget to check the dust collector.

Eleven to three. Two of us mill the Yonge walnut. The third carpenter installs hardware on the Davisville boxes — under-mount slides, soft-close hinges, the cup pulls that the homeowner picked from a sample of fourteen. The pulls are the most expensive small item in the kitchen on a per-cabinet basis. People are always surprised by that.

Three-thirty to five. Spray booth. Two doors and a panel for a Leaside project that has been sitting in the rack for a week waiting on the colour-match approval from the homeowner. The approval came in at lunch on Monday. The doors get a tinted sealer, get sanded back, and will get the topcoat on Wednesday morning.

Wednesday.

Site day. One of us is out at the Bayview install from seven-thirty until two. Three uppers, the range hood enclosure, the pantry door. The hood enclosure does not fit. It misses by a quarter inch on the left wall return because the drywaller, who finished the wall after we measured, built the corner out three sixteenths farther than the drawing said. We do not get angry. We bring a pull-saw, scribe the cabinet to the wall, take a fresh quarter inch off the back rail, and the cabinet lands at noon. The lost time is forty-five minutes. The reason we do not get angry is that this happens on roughly one in four installs and arguing about it makes it take longer.

Back at the shop, the third carpenter sprays the Leaside topcoats and starts on the Yonge cut list.

Thursday.

The longest single bench day of the week. Eight-thirty to five-thirty on the Yonge walnut. Doors are five-piece slab construction with a quarter-inch reveal at each stile. Drawer fronts are matched-grain pairs from a single board so that the grain runs uninterrupted across the bank. This is the kind of detail that takes about ninety extra minutes per bank and that nobody, not even the homeowner, will consciously notice once it is installed. They will, however, feel that the kitchen looks "calm" in a way they cannot articulate. The calm is the matched grain. The calm is the ninety minutes.

  1. Match pairs at the saw before they go to the planer. Mark every board with the orientation arrow in pencil on the show face. Once the orientation is wrong, the cabinet stops being calm.
  2. Cut all five drawer fronts for a single bank from one board. If the board is not long enough to yield five pieces, choose two boards from the same flitch, not from the same species. The species does not match. The flitch does.
  3. Rest the cut pieces overnight before glue-up. Walnut moves more than people think in the twelve hours after a rip cut. If you glue immediately, the panel goes out of square by Monday.

Friday.

Half-bench, half-clean. Mornings on Friday are when we finish whatever is still mid-step from Thursday. The Yonge doors glue up at ten. The Leaside topcoats go out the door at noon, packed in moving blankets, loaded into our trailer for a Monday-morning install at Bayview-and-Eg.

— The Friday Rule —

Nothing leaves the shop on a Friday afternoon. Anything we install on a Monday gets loaded Friday at noon. Anything we still have at four o'clock Friday gets put back in the rack until Monday morning. The Friday-afternoon delivery is the one that gets dropped, gets backed into, or arrives at the site with a chip you can see from six feet away. Every shop learns this rule the hard way. We learned it in 2019.

Twelve-thirty to four. Shop clean. We sweep, we empty the dust collector, we sharpen what needs sharpening, we order what needs ordering for Monday morning. The clean is not a frill. The clean is the only reason a Monday-morning bench is productive. If we leave the shop dirty on Friday, we lose ninety minutes on Monday looking for things and apologising to each other. The Friday clean costs us forty-five minutes. The math is honest.

Four o'clock the lights go off. The compressor goes off. The doors lock. Five days. Four jobs moved forward. One planer blade. One install that needed to be scribed. One spray booth running clean. The week looked exactly like the week before and exactly like the week after, and that is the whole point. Predictable weeks build predictable kitchens.
