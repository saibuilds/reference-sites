The shaker door, revisited.

A door style that was supposed to be simple has quietly become the most technical thing we build. Three small changes that took us a decade to learn.
BY SAI NATHAN

Eight out of every ten kitchens we build now are some flavour of shaker door. It is, by any honest accounting, the dominant Toronto kitchen of the 2020s. Painted-white shaker. Painted-bone shaker. Soft-green shaker. The occasional natural-rift shaker. The shape is so universal that homeowners forget the door has any history at all and the cabinetmakers, frankly, sometimes forget too. The shaker door is supposed to be the simple one. A stile, a rail, a flat centre panel. Four pieces of wood and a corner joint. Easy. Except after the first hundred we built we realised the shaker door is actually the hardest cabinet door we make, because it has nowhere to hide.

Why simple is hard.

A profiled door — a beaded inset, an arched panel, an applied moulding — carries its own decoration. The shadow line of the bead distracts the eye from a stile joint that is half a degree out of square. The arch in the upper rail draws the eye away from the panel reveal. The applied moulding hides the cope. None of those forgivenesses exist on a shaker door. Every line is straight. Every joint is visible. Every panel reveal is the same width all the way around or it is wrong. If the stile-to-rail joint opens up by even a hair after the first heating season, the door looks broken. It does not look "rustic." It looks broken.

The three things we changed.

We built shaker doors for a decade more or less the way we were taught to. Five-piece construction. Quarter-inch ply panel floating in a groove. Mortise-and-tenon corners. The doors looked fine on day one. Roughly six of every hundred came back inside of two years with a hairline gap at the stile-rail joint, or a panel rattle, or a stile bow. Six per cent is not a catastrophe but it is enough that every shop foreman knows the number. Three years ago we changed three small things and the return rate dropped to under one per cent. Here is what we changed.

  1. We stopped using ply for the centre panel and started using veneered medium-density fibreboard with a hard-wax surface seal on both faces before assembly. Ply moves seasonally with humidity even at quarter inch; veneered MDF does not. The seal on both faces is non-negotiable — if the back is unsealed, the panel cups towards the back over the first heating season.
  2. We switched the corner joint from a glued mortise-and-tenon to a doweled-and-glued cope-and-stick with two eight-millimetre beech dowels at each corner. The cope-and-stick gives us a flat continuous shoulder line. The dowels give us mechanical reinforcement that does not rely on the glue alone surviving a Toronto winter. The joint is invisible. The strength is in the dowels.
  3. We undersize the panel groove by half a millimetre and use four small foam space balls in the groove rather than gluing the panel in. The panel floats. It moves seasonally without stressing the frame. The space balls keep it centred so it does not rattle. The panel-rattle problem disappears.

The shaker door is a quiet door. The whole point is that nothing distracts you. The whole craft is in making sure nothing distracts you, including the door itself in year five.

The reveal nobody talks about.

The most-fought-over millimetre on the door is the reveal between the centre panel and the inner edge of the frame. The factory standard is roughly four millimetres on three sides and six millimetres on the bottom rail to allow for seasonal movement. The factory standard looks heavy. We use 2.5 millimetres on three sides and four on the bottom. It is a tighter look, more like the original Shaker pieces in the New Lebanon community-house museum, and it reads as quieter and more architectural. You can only get away with the tighter reveal if you have absolutely controlled the panel material — which is point one above — and the frame stability, which is point two. If you tighten the reveal on a ply-panelled mortise-and-tenon door, it will bind by August. Do not do it.

The finish question.

The other quiet difference between a good shaker door and a great one is the finish. A spray-applied two-coat catalysed enamel will look perfect for about six years and then it will start to show micro-cracks at the stile-rail intersection because the enamel is harder than the wood underneath and the wood moves underneath. A water-based polyurethane will not micro-crack but will yellow slightly under direct sunlight over ten years. A pre-catalysed lacquer is the middle ground we use most often — it is harder than poly, more flexible than enamel, and it ages to a slightly warmer tone that most clients prefer over the cooler enamel finish anyway. The fourth option, which is hand-painted oil enamel with three coats and twenty-four-hour cures, is the one we recommend for kitchens that the client expects to live in for thirty years, and it is the one nobody chooses because it adds two weeks to the schedule.

— A Bench Note —

The single best test of a shaker door is to install one in a kitchen, leave it for a year, then take the door off the hinges and lay it on the bench. If the frame is still perfectly flat against the bench — no twist, no cup — the construction worked. We do this with the first door off every kitchen we build, on the second anniversary of the install, as a quality check on the rest of the run. We have lost two hours of bench time once. We have caught one batch of bad panels before it bit us.

What the simple door taught us.

Twelve years of building shaker doors taught us that the apparent simplicity of a thing is almost always inversely related to how hard it is to do well. The Roman columns, the Eames chair, the Japanese tea bowl — the things that look like nothing took the longest. Cabinet doors are no exception. The shaker door has hidden, behind its plain face, almost every advance our shop has made in joinery, panel selection, finish chemistry, and reveal control in the past decade. The fact that the homeowner cannot see any of that work is, in some real way, the point. The door should not announce itself. The door should make the room quiet enough for the rest of the kitchen to do the talking.

If you are about to commission a kitchen and a builder tries to tell you that shaker is the boring choice, find another builder. Shaker is not the boring choice. Shaker is the choice that gives away whether they know what they are doing.
