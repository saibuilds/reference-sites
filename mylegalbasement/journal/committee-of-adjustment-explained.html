Committee of Adjustment, demystified.

What a Toronto minor-variance hearing actually looks like, who shows up, and why the rumours about it being scary are mostly out of date.
BY SAI NATHAN

Roughly one in three of the suites we build in Toronto ends up at the Committee of Adjustment. The phone call where I tell a client that is, almost without exception, the most uncomfortable phone call in the project. People hear "minor variance" and assume that we have done something wrong, that the neighbours will object, that the project is in jeopardy. None of that is usually true. This is what actually happens, written down for the homeowner who has just been told it is happening to them.

The Committee of Adjustment is a quasi-judicial panel of five appointed members that sits in each of the four community council districts. They meet roughly twice a month. They hear small variances from the zoning bylaw — the kinds of small numbers that a basement conversion sometimes runs into, like a ceiling height that is three inches shy of code-of-the-day, or a rear-yard parking space that is half a metre too close to the lot line, or a side entrance whose stair landing extends thirty centimetres beyond the permitted projection. These are not architectural sins. They are the residue of building a 1955 house to a 2025 bylaw.

What the hearing looks like.

The hearing happens in a city committee room at one of the four civic centres. Etobicoke at Burnhamthorpe. North York at Mel Lastman Square. Scarborough at McCowan. Toronto and East York at City Hall on Queen. The room is small, panelled, fluorescent-lit, and not at all theatrical. There is a panel table with the five committee members, a small projection screen, two chairs for the applicant and the applicant's planner or builder, a chair for the city planner who is staffing the hearing, and three or four rows of public seating at the back.

The hearing is scheduled in fifteen-minute blocks. You will not get fifteen minutes. The committee will give you four. The applicant speaks first. Their planner, or in our case usually one of us, gives a one-minute description of what is being asked for and why. The city planner reads a one-paragraph staff report. The chair then asks if any neighbour wants to speak. In a routine basement-suite application, no one shows up. The committee deliberates aloud for ninety seconds. They vote. They move to the next file. You are back in the parking lot in twenty minutes.

Who actually shows up.

This is the part homeowners get wrong. Almost no one shows up to other people's hearings. In four years of representing applicants, I have had a neighbour appear to speak against a basement suite exactly three times. Two of those were the same neighbour, on two different files, on the same street. The third was a councillor's constituency assistant who, on inspection, was there to support, not oppose.

The hearing is not the courtroom drama the homeowner is imagining. It is fifteen minutes of polite municipal administration in a small panelled room with bad lighting and good coffee at the back.

The three things we do in the eight weeks before.

What actually matters is what happens between the day we file and the day of the hearing. The hearing itself is the formality. The preparation is the work.

  1. We walk the adjacent properties — the two side neighbours, the rear neighbour, and the two opposite-side neighbours across the street — with a single-page letter from the homeowner that describes, plainly, what is being built. We do this in person, with the homeowner, on a Saturday. Almost every objection we have ever seen at hearing was a neighbour who first heard about the project from the city's mailed notice.
  2. We address the city planner's pre-circulation comments before the hearing notice goes out. The planner will almost always have one or two technical concerns — a drawing label that is unclear, a setback dimension that does not quite match the survey. Fix those in writing two weeks before the hearing, and the staff report reads as supportive on the day.
  3. We prepare the homeowner for the room. We tell them what door to walk through, where to sit, whether to speak. We tell them that the committee members are usually planners or retired council staff, not lawyers, and that they will be polite. We tell them to wear what they would wear to a parent-teacher meeting.

What the committee actually cares about.

The four tests the committee applies are public and short. Is the variance minor? Is it desirable for the appropriate development of the land? Does it maintain the general intent and purpose of the bylaw? Does it maintain the general intent and purpose of the official plan? In a routine basement suite, the answer to all four is almost always yes, and the staff report says so. The committee reads the staff report before you walk in. If the staff report supports the application, you are starting at four-nil. You have to lose four votes to lose the file. Almost no homeowner loses four votes.

— A Quiet Note —

The single most useful sentence we have ever heard in a committee hearing was the chair saying, after a thirty-second deliberation: "this looks like a fairly housekeeping matter, all in favour." That is what most of these files actually sound like.

If it does go badly.

Roughly one in twenty applications gets refused or deferred. Refusal almost always comes with a clear reason in the decision letter — a setback that is too far from the bylaw, a use that the committee felt was actually a major variance in disguise. Deferral is the more common bad outcome; it usually means the committee wants more information, or wants the applicant to talk to a specific neighbour who showed up. In a deferral you re-file with a one-page response, the city plans you back into the next hearing four to six weeks out, and the file typically passes on the second pass. We have never — touch wood — lost a basement suite at the appeal stage that followed.

If you take one thing from this piece, take this. The Committee of Adjustment is not the obstacle that the contractor down the street made it sound like, and it is not the boss-fight the renovation forums make it sound like either. It is a small panel of mostly-friendly people who have read your file and who want to give you twenty minutes of polite municipal attention before they vote yes and go to lunch. We will sit beside you in the room. You will be fine.
