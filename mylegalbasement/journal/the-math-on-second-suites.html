The math on second suites, honest version.

Three spreadsheets from three real clients, with the lines the brochures leave out: vacancy, maintenance, and the tax bill in year four.
BY SAI NATHAN

Every basement-suite brochure I have ever read uses the same arithmetic. Build cost divided by gross monthly rent equals payback. The number always comes out somewhere between six and eight years. The number is a fiction. The number is wrong in the same three ways every single time, and the three ways are not small. They are the difference between a build that pays for itself in nine years and one that quietly bleeds the homeowner for fifteen. What follows is the actual math from three of the suites we finished in the past eighteen months, with the lines the brochure does not put in.

Client one · East York semi, two-bedroom suite.

Build cost: $187,000 including the side-entrance walkout, the underpinning, the appliance package, and the city permit fees. Gross rent: $2,450 a month, which is what the suite leased for in the first thirty days. The brochure math says payback in six years, four months. Here is what the brochure math leaves out. Property-tax reassessment in year two: the city's MPAC re-assessment increased the assessed value of the house by $112,000, which added $1,640 to the annual tax bill. Insurance increase for a converted dwelling: $720 a year. Maintenance reserve at three per cent of gross rent: $880 a year. Vacancy reserve at one month every twenty-four months: $1,225 a year amortized. Property management when the homeowner decided in year two that she did not want to be the landlord: ten per cent of gross, $2,940 a year.

Net rent, after all of that, in year two onward: $22,995 a year. Payback on $187,000 of build cost: eight years, one month. Not six years, four months. Still a good number. Just not the brochure number.

Client two · Scarborough bungalow, one-bedroom suite.

Build cost: $124,000. Lower because there was no underpinning — the original 1962 basement was already seven-foot-two with bench-footings in good condition. Gross rent: $1,725. Brochure payback: six years exactly. Here is where the math gets interesting and where the brochure goes the most wrong.

The brochure assumes the suite is rented every month for ten years. No suite is rented every month for ten years. Vacancy is real. Turnover is real. The two weeks between tenants is the line that breaks the spreadsheet.

This client's actual occupancy in the first three years has been 91 per cent. That is not bad. That is, in fact, slightly above the Scarborough average for one-bedroom basement units. But 91 per cent occupancy at $1,725 gross is $18,820 a year, not $20,700. Net of everything — tax bump $1,180, insurance $640, maintenance $565, no property management because the client manages it himself — the suite returns $16,435 a year. Payback on $124,000: seven years, six months. Still excellent. Still not six years.

Client three · Riverdale semi, garden suite.

This is the one where the math gets hard. Build cost: $342,000 for a 740-square-foot garden suite at the rear of the lot, fully detached, on its own meter. Gross rent: $2,950. Brochure payback: nine years, eight months.

Three lines the brochure forgot. One: the development charges and parkland levies on a garden suite in the City of Toronto added $14,200 to the project cost that the original quote did not include — we caught it before the homeowner signed but it is the single most common surprise on a garden-suite file. Two: the property-tax reassessment on a garden suite is more aggressive than a basement reassessment, because the city treats it as a separately-rateable second dwelling. The increase on this file was $2,810 a year. Three: garden suites depreciate cosmetically faster than basements, because they are exposed to weather; the maintenance reserve on a garden suite should be five per cent of gross rent, not three.

  1. Add the development charges before you sign, not after. Ask your builder for the city's most recent garden-suite DC schedule in writing. If they cannot produce it in twenty-four hours, that is the answer.
  2. Run the MPAC reassessment line at fifteen to twenty per cent of build cost amortized over five years. That is the order of magnitude. Do not run it at zero.
  3. Add a real vacancy line. One month every two years is the floor, not the ceiling. If you cannot stomach a fourteen-month vacancy in year six, do not build the suite.

With all three of those lines in the spreadsheet, the Riverdale garden suite pays back in eleven years and four months. Still a good investment. Still adds roughly $380,000 to the appraised value of the property on a refinance. But not the nine years and eight months the brochure promised.

— The Line We Always Add —

The honest payback on a Toronto second suite, with realistic vacancy and a real tax line, is nine to twelve years. Anyone telling you six is either selling you something or has not built one. Both numbers are still good. The twelve-year suite still doubles your money before the mortgage is paid off. It just does not do it in six.

What the spreadsheet does not capture.

The three clients above are running fine. None of them regret the build. Two of them have already started a second project — one is converting the garage into a third unit, one is helping her sister underpin a Davisville semi. The reason they are happy is not the spreadsheet. The reason they are happy is that the suite gave them an option. The option to retire one year earlier. The option to subsidize a child's first year of university without dipping into RRSPs. The option to host an aging parent without finishing a mother-in-law suite over the garage. The spreadsheet does not have a line for "options," but the options are usually why the spreadsheet was worth running in the first place. We just think the spreadsheet should be honest before you sign the contract, not nine years in.
