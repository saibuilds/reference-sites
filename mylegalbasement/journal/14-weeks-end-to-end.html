What fourteen weeks looks like, day by day.

A real East York second-suite schedule, broken into the days we count and the days we lose. No marketing gantt charts, no aspirational arrows.
BY SAI NATHAN

The brochures all say twelve weeks. The honest answer is fourteen, sometimes sixteen, and the four weeks of difference are almost never the part of the schedule the homeowner is worried about. The slow weeks are not framing or drywall. They are the weeks before a single carpenter sets foot on the site, and the two days at the end that nobody bills for. This is the actual day-by-day from a 2025 build we just finished on Coxwell — a two-bedroom basement suite in a 1948 East York semi, separate side entrance, full underpinning, in-floor heat in the bathroom. Fourteen weeks and four days, gate to gate.

Weeks one and two · the part nobody photographs.

Day one is not demolition. Day one is a site visit with the structural engineer and a measure-up by our drafter. The engineer is on site for forty-five minutes. The drafter is on site for three hours. Nothing happens to the house in week one except a tape measure, a laser, and four small core samples through the basement slab to confirm what is under it. We learn on this day whether the original foundation is fieldstone, poured concrete, or block, and that single fact changes the next eight weeks of the schedule.

Week two is permit drawings. Our drafter produces architectural at quarter-inch scale, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing schematics, and a structural package that the engineer stamps. By Friday of week two the package is uploaded to the city's Application Status portal. The homeowner does not see anything change on their house. They sometimes call us mid-week to ask whether we have forgotten about them. We have not. The work is happening on a screen.

Weeks three through six · the city.

Toronto Building takes between three and six weeks to issue a permit on a second-suite file with no variances. With a minor variance — the situation we wrote about last week — add four to six more. On the Coxwell file the permit landed on Tuesday of week four, which is fast for the East York district office. The two weeks between submission and approval are not idle weeks for us; we are sourcing the bathroom tile, the kitchen cabinet boxes, and the egress window frame, all of which have lead times that we want to start before permit so that they land in the driveway in week seven.

The schedule lives or dies on whether materials are sitting in the driveway the morning the trades arrive. If they are not, the trade leaves and we lose two days getting them back.

Weeks seven through ten · the loud part.

This is the part homeowners imagine when they imagine a renovation. The skip arrives Monday morning of week seven. Demolition is two days. Underpinning, when needed, is the longest single trade on the file — sequential pour windows, one bay at a time, with a forty-eight-hour cure between bays. On a typical 1948 East York semi the underpinning sequence is fourteen working days. We use the underpinning weeks to run rough plumbing in the slab and to frame the partition walls that are not affected by the dig.

Week ten is the inspection week. Framing inspection on Tuesday, plumbing rough-in on Wednesday, electrical rough-in on Thursday. We schedule them in that order on purpose; the framer is on site through Thursday so that if the inspector flags anything, the saw is still on site. We have never had to call a framer back to a site in 2025. This is not luck. This is one carpenter who has read the OBC three times.

Weeks eleven through fourteen · the part that decides the rental.

The finishes are what the future tenant will see. The finishes are also where every schedule on every project quietly slips. There are three reasons a finish week slips, and they are the same three reasons every time.

  1. The wrong tile arrives. The supplier delivers the right SKU but the wrong dye lot, and the tile setter (correctly) refuses to set it. We rebook the tile, lose four days, and tell the homeowner on a Tuesday evening.
  2. The cabinet boxes arrive damaged. A corner has been crushed in transit and the door will not close square. We re-order one box, lose six working days, and improvise an installation order so that the kitchen island goes in before the wall run.
  3. The final electrical inspection fails on a single junction box that the electrician forgot to label. The inspector cannot pass the file until the label is on. The electrician is on another site for two days. We lose two days.

None of these are heroic stories. None of them make it onto a brochure. All of them are the actual reason your basement-suite project is going to take fourteen weeks and four days instead of twelve weeks flat.

— The Honest Note —

The single biggest predictor of an on-time finish is whether the homeowner makes their finish selections during week three, not week eight. The cabinets cannot ship until the door style is locked. The tile cannot ship until the grout colour is locked. If you tell us your selections in week eight, we will finish in week sixteen. Every time.

The two days at the end nobody bills for.

The final occupancy inspection passes on a Wednesday. The keys are handed to the homeowner on a Thursday morning. There are two more days after that. One is for the punch list — the small dozen items that the homeowner notices once the trades are gone and the suite is quiet. A door that swings half a degree wide. A baseboard joint that needs a second pass of caulk. A switch plate that sits three millimetres proud. The second day is the day we come back six weeks later to check the door swings again after the wood has settled, to walk the suite with the first tenant, and to drop off the binder — warranties, manuals, inspection records — that turns the basement from a renovation into an asset. We do not bill for the second day. We never have.

Fourteen weeks and four days, gate to gate. The brochure says twelve. The truth is somewhere in the middle of those four extra days that nobody warned you about, and the only way to make those days disappear is to spend twenty more minutes on a Saturday in week three picking the tile.
