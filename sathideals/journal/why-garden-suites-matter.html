Why garden suites are quietly reshaping Toronto's inner suburbs.

A new permit class, a generational handover, and a slow softening of the single-family lot. The change is gentler than the headlines suggest.
BY SAI NATHAN

The Toronto headlines about as-of-right multiplexes are loud. The actual change on the ground, where my clients live, is quieter, and it is happening at the rear of the lot. Garden suites — the small, self-contained second dwellings the city now permits behind detached and semi-detached homes — have become, in the last eighteen months, the most consequential thing happening to the postwar bungalow belt. I do not think that is hyperbole. I think most of the city has missed it.

The neighbourhoods I am watching are the ones that grew up between roughly 1948 and 1962: the western half of Long Branch, the deeper parts of East York above O'Connor, the long blocks of north Scarborough between Birchmount and Pharmacy, the south end of North York where the lots still run a hundred and twenty deep. In these areas the original buyers were veterans and first-generation Canadians who bought their plots when Toronto was a small city. Their children inherited the houses. Their grandchildren cannot afford them. The garden suite is, among other things, the financial instrument that lets the grandchildren stay.

What changed in the bylaw.

The 2022 zoning amendments allowed a detached secondary dwelling on virtually any residential lot in the city, with relatively modest setback, height, and gross-floor-area limits. The 2024 follow-up doubled the permitted GFA on the deeper lots and clarified the rear-yard coverage formula in a way that, for the first time, made the math work on a standard fifty-foot frontage. The city now processes these as a single combined permit. The wait time, when I last checked with the planner I work with most often in East York, is running about eleven weeks.

None of this is news. What is news, perhaps, is how the appetite has matured. Three years ago the inquiries I was getting about garden suites were from speculators — investor-buyers asking whether they could put two of them up the laneway and create a four-unit cash-flow play. Today the inquiries are almost entirely from existing homeowners and their adult children. The use case is multi-generational, not extractive. That changes the design brief. It also changes what we look for when we buy.

The garden suite is the financial instrument that lets the grandchildren stay. It is, quietly, a tool for keeping families on the same street across three generations.

What we now look for in a buying lot.

When we evaluate a postwar bungalow today for a client who has any interest in long-term flexibility, the rear-yard analysis happens before the kitchen walkthrough. The questions I ask my own clients have changed. They are now, in order:

  1. Is the lot at least one hundred and ten feet deep, with a usable rear access of at least three feet on one side? Without rear access the garden suite is technically possible but practically miserable to build, because every cubic yard of concrete arrives through the house.
  2. Is there mature canopy at the back of the lot that the homeowner will not want to lose? Two-thirds of the projects we have seen abandoned at design phase are killed not by zoning but by a hundred-year-old oak the family loves.
  3. Is the existing house comfortable to live in for the ten years it will take for the garden suite to make sense? The garden suite is not a renovation. It is a separate dwelling. The main house has to stand on its own first.

Where the math actually pencils.

The investor question of does it pay for itself deserves a real answer rather than a brochure answer. In my experience, on a 110-by-25 garden suite built to the permit floor and rented at current East York one-bedroom rates, the payback period sits somewhere between nine and twelve years if you funded the build from refinanced equity, and somewhere between fourteen and seventeen years if you funded it from cash. Those numbers are not exciting. They are also not the point. The point is that the suite makes a long-term hold of the main house meaningfully cheaper in year fifteen, which is when the children who paid too much for their own first house are starting to ask whether they can move in with their parents.

— A Quiet Pattern —

The two garden-suite projects from our practice that have closed best, in 2025, were both inhabited by a parent or an in-law within twelve months of occupancy, not by a market tenant. The cash-on-cash return is lower. The household balance sheet is healthier.

What this means for what we list.

If you are a seller in one of these belts, the lot itself is now more important than it was a decade ago. The exact depth of your rear yard, the position of your existing accessory structures, and the fact that your driveway runs all the way to the back of the property — these used to be neutral facts in our listing photographs. They are now value drivers we lead with. Two of the last four houses we have listed have gone to buyers who walked the rear yard before they walked the main floor. One of them did not enter the basement at all.

The headlines call this a housing crisis solved by density. That is not exactly what I see. What I see is a generation of Toronto families that owned their houses outright, are gently being asked to share the lot, and are finding the answer easier than the headlines suggest. The change is quiet. It is also, I think, durable.
