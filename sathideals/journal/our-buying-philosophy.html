Our buying philosophy · slow looks, long holds.

Why we walk every house twice before we write, why we never represent two offers on the same street, and why we still write our offers by hand.
BY SAI NATHAN

People sometimes ask what makes our practice different. The honest answer is that very little of what we do is novel. The work itself is old. We walk houses. We listen to sellers. We write offers. What is unusual is what we have decided we will not do, and how slowly we have decided to do the things we will. This piece is an attempt to be clear about both.

Sathi and I have been writing offers as a two-person partnership since 2017. The first year we did fourteen transactions. Last year we did eleven. That is not because the market got smaller. It is because we made it harder for ourselves to take a file. A client who has not asked us at least one difficult question about their own intentions tends to be a client we are not the right fit for, and we say so out loud now, where eight years ago we would have politely accepted the listing and quietly suffered.

We walk every house twice.

The first walk is theatre. The seller has staged the rooms, the agent has burned a cedar candle in the entryway, the music is on, the bedside table has a book opened to page seventy-eight. None of that is dishonest. It is simply not the house you will own. The house you will own is the one we see on the second walk, on a Tuesday morning at nine, with the seller's agent yawning in the kitchen and the dog from next door barking through the fence, and the kitchen tap that ticks when the dishwasher is on, and the seam in the ceiling above the principal bedroom where last winter's ice dam ran.

We almost never write on the first walk. We will write on the second. Occasionally we will write on the third, in which case we are usually in a multiple-offer situation and have already decided privately what number is the highest we will go to before we begin. The discipline of writing the number on a separate sheet of paper, before the offer presentation begins, has saved more than one of our clients more than the brokerage fee.

We do not represent two offers on the same street.

This one costs us money. It also means that, by the end of a deal, our client knows for certain that they have not been bid against by another of our files. It also means that the listing agents in the neighbourhoods we work in have learned, over time, that an offer with our name on it is one offer, not two, and that the number on the cover sheet is the number we mean.

We still write our offers by hand.

Not literally with a pen. The OREA form gets typed like everyone else's. But the cover letter that goes with it — the page that explains who the buyer is, why this house, what they intend to do with it, who their lender is, how long they have been looking — is written by Sathi or by me, in the evening, after the second walk. We do not use a template. We do not paste in last week's letter. We write the buyer's actual story, in three or four short paragraphs, because in a market where every offer arrives at the same hour on a Tuesday with the same number across the top, the cover letter is the one piece of the file the seller actually reads.

Sellers respond to it. We have lost houses by a hundred thousand dollars because the seller wanted to know who would live there next. We have also won houses by a thousand dollars for the same reason. The cover letter is, in some sense, the entire job.

Three commitments we make to every client.

  1. We will never advise you to write on a house we have not walked with you, in person, at least once. We do not write on virtual tours. We do not write on a friend-of-a-friend assignment. If we have not been in the rooms, we are not the right firm for that deal.
  2. We will tell you, in writing, what we think the house is worth, in our own honest opinion, on the same day we present the listing. That number may be lower than the list price. We will still tell you. You can do whatever you want with the information.
  3. If we believe the file is wrong for the long-term life you have described to us, we will say so clearly, once, in plain language, with our reasoning. We will then do whatever you decide. The job of the agent is to give the client the truth and then let the client choose.
— A Note on Pace —

The single most expensive mistake we have watched buyers make in eight years of practice is not paying too much for the wrong house. It is rushing on the right house. We will not let you rush. If that costs us a deal, it costs us a deal.

Why we hold the relationship longer than the transaction.

The closing is not the end of our work. Most of our clients hear from us once a quarter for the next decade. We write a private letter, never published, that goes to perhaps thirty households, with our honest read on the market and a short list of off-market houses we have walked that we think one of those households might want to know about. The letter is mailed, on paper. It costs us several afternoons a year to write. It is the reason that when one of those households eventually moves, they tend to move with us.

If any of this sounds slow, that is because it is. We are not the right firm for a buyer who needs to write by the weekend on a house they have not yet walked. We are the right firm for a buyer who can wait. Lawrence Park rewards patience. So, in our experience, does almost everything in this city worth owning.
